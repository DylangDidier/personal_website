Haddy Handheld Defect Logger

PythonHardware IntegrationCAD / 3D Printing
Overview

Quality control on the manufacturing floor previously required operators to manage a camera, clipboard, and measuring tools simultaneously. To solve this, I engineered a custom 3D-printed smart data logger that consolidates this workflow. By pointing a targeting laser and pulling a trigger, operators can capture an image that automatically calculates defect dimensions and syncs directly to the cloud.

My Contributions
  • Hardware Integration: Interfaced a high-resolution camera, Time-of-Flight (ToF) distance sensor, targeting laser, and touchscreen with a Raspberry Pi 4 using I2C and GPIO protocols.
  • Mechanical Design: Designed a custom, durable enclosure using CAD software and manufactured it with high-strength ABS filament to withstand the physical demands of an industrial shop floor.
  • User Interface: Developed a touchscreen GUI in Python (CustomTkinter) to allow operators to easily input material types and categorize defects on the fly.
  • Image Processing & Telemetry: Wrote Python logic that utilizes ToF sensor data to calculate the exact physical dimensions of the defect. Automated a pipeline to embed a metadata footer—containing measurements, material data, and timestamps—directly onto the saved JPEG file.
  • Power System & Timing: Integrated a step-down converter to power the system using hot-swappable DeWalt power tool batteries. Solved image glare by programming micro-delays to disable the targeting laser a fraction of a second before the camera shutter releases.
  • Sheets Integration: Uses a background webhook to instantly send data to a Google Sheet, automatically converting image filenames into direct, clickable Smart Chips linked to the Drive photo.
Results
  • Streamlined a multi-step quality assurance process into a single, user-friendly point-and-shoot tool, significantly reducing logging time.
  • Eliminated human error in defect measurement by automating the area calculation using real-time distance sensor data.
  • Configured automated, headless cloud syncing (via Rclone) to Google Drive, giving the engineering team instant visibility into shop-floor defects.

Auburn–Regions Data Hackathon: Loan Default Prediction (3rd Place)

PythonLightGBMSHAPscikit-learnCredit Risk
Overview

A three-person team project with Dane Miller and Vaughn Miller. Working from a dataset of 45,000 loan applications, along with each applicant's past accounts and prior loan requests, we estimated every applicant's chance of serious payment trouble and turned those estimates into a lending policy that approves, declines, or offers a smaller loan. Because lending decisions are regulated, the model also had to be fair and able to explain every decline. We placed 3rd.

My Contributions
  • Gradient-Boosted Challenger: Trained a LightGBM model in Python on 30,000 training applications with 5-fold cross-validation and early stopping, using monotone constraints so each input can only push risk in the direction that makes business sense.
  • SHAP Explanations: Used SHAP to break each applicant's risk score into how much every input pushed it up or down, turning the biggest upward pushes into the specific decline reasons fair-lending rules (Regulation B) require.
  • Benchmarking: The LightGBM model edged out the team's 10-input logistic regression scorecard, with a cross-validated AUC of 0.745 vs. 0.742 (0.765 vs. 0.762 on the held-out validation set).
  • Choosing Clarity Over Accuracy: We chose the scorecard as the final model, giving up 0.003 AUC for a simple points system that explains every decline. Its predicted default rates also held up at the decision cutoff (14.3% predicted vs. 14.1% actual).
  • Profit-Based Lending Policy: Co-designed a policy that weighs each applicant's expected profit against expected loss, and offers a smaller loan to applicants who are too risky at the full amount.
Results
  • Placed 3rd in the Auburn–Regions Data Hackathon.
  • The final policy lends to 69% of applicants: 65% in full at a 6.2% default rate, down from 12.6% if everyone were approved, plus 4% at a reduced amount.
  • Kept protected traits like age, marital status, and household size out of the model entirely, and checked outcomes across groups only after every decision was made.
  • Shipped a fully reproducible pipeline: one command rebuilds the predictions in under a minute, backed by 322 automated checks covering data quality, leakage, calibration, and fairness.

Bitcoin Alpha Trust Network Analysis & Graph Modeling System

Network ScienceGraph ModelingCommunity DetectionStatistical Analysis
Overview

For my Social Network Analysis course, I built a network science pipeline to explore how trust and reputation actually form in a decentralized crypto marketplace. The project analyzes the Bitcoin Alpha trust network: 3,783 users and 24,186 rated transactions.

My Contributions
  • Directed, Weighted Graph Model: Modeled the network as a directed, weighted graph, since trust isn't automatically mutual. Someone can rate you highly without you doing the same in return.
  • Core & Community Structure: Used K-core decomposition to find a dense 34-layer "inner circle" of power users, then applied Louvain and Walktrap community detection to map out separate trading clusters within the network.
  • Power-Law Testing: Tested whether the network follows a power-law (scale-free) structure using Maximum Likelihood Estimation, finding a heavy-tailed degree distribution (α = 2.7, xmin = 32) with a bootstrap goodness-of-fit p-value of 0.08.
  • Null-Model Benchmarking: To confirm these patterns weren't random, benchmarked the real network against two generative baselines: a randomized null model (rewired edges, same degree sequence) and a Holme-Kim model, measuring how much more clustered and small-world the real data was.
  • Inequality Analysis: Used Lorenz curves to measure how unevenly influence and connections are spread across users.
Results
  • Found that a small number of users hold a disproportionate share of the network's connections.
  • Uncovered a serious structural bottleneck: the maximum betweenness centrality was 246x the network average, meaning the platform's stability essentially rests on a small handful of gatekeepers.
  • Identified a dual behavioral pattern among elite users: negative degree assortativity (−0.16) shows they actively vouch for and connect with newcomers, while positive trust-attribute assortativity (0.098) shows they keep their highest-value transactions within a tight, high-trust core.
